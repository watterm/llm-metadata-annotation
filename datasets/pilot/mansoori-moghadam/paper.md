## KEY POINTS

- ROS produced by monocytes and macrophages are essential to maintain intestinal homeostasis.
- The wildling model substantially improves preclinical modeling of the complex IBD phenotype in CGD.

The controlled development of cellular intestinal immunity in the face of dynamic microbiota emergence constitutes a major challenge in very early life and is a bottleneck for sustained growth and well-being. Early-onset inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) represents an extreme disturbance of intestinal immunity. It is a hallmark and often the first manifestation of chronic granulomatous disease (CGD), caused by inborn defects in the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase 2 (NOX2) in phagocytes and thus the failure to produce reactive oxygen species (ROS). However, in contrast to the known role of ROS in antimicrobial defense, the mechanisms underlying intestinal immunopathology in CGD remain enigmatic. This is partly due to the incomplete recapitulation of the CGD-IBD phenotype in established mouse models. We found that mice deficient in the NOX2 subunits $p 47^{\text {phox }}$ or $g p 91^{\text {phox }}$ showed similar baseline disturbances in lamina propria macrophage differentiation but responded differently to chemically induced colitis. Although $p 47^{\text {phox }}$ - and $g p 91^{\text {phox }}$-deficient mice differed markedly in microbiota composition, crossfostering failed to equalize discrepant IBD phenotypes and microbiota, pointing at extremely early and functionally important microbiota fixation under specific pathogen-free housing conditions. In contrast, neonatal acquisition of a complex wild-mouse microbiota triggered spontaneous IBD, granuloma formation, and secondary sepsis with intestinal pathogens in both NOX2deficient mouse lines, which was in part dependent on NOX2 in intestinal macrophages. Thus, in experimental CGD, the aberrant development of tissue immunity and microbiota are closely intertwined immediately after birth.

## Introduction

Chronic granulomatous disease (CGD) is an inborn error of immunity caused by dysfunction in one of the subunits of the nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate oxidase, which is predominantly expressed in phagocytes (NOX2), and is associated with a lack of reactive oxygen species (ROS). ${ }^{1}$ Next to severe bacterial and fungal infections, patients with CGD commonly show inflammation in peripheral tissues. ${ }^{2,3}$ In particular, CGD is associated with severe early-onset inflammatory bowel disease (IBD) in $\sim 50 \%$ of patients, ${ }^{4}$ which interferes with general health. Overall, stem cell transplantation is the only curative therapeutic option for both susceptibility to infection and inflammatory complications in

CGD. ${ }^{5}$ Therefore, improved mechanistic understanding of IBD in CGD is of great medical importance. In IBD, tissue macrophages potently produce inflammatory cytokines, indicating their central pathogenic role. ${ }^{6}$ The function of intestinal macrophages and other tissue immune cells is modulated by the microbiota or its derivatives, ${ }^{7}$ as indicated by aberrations in cellular homing and function in germ-free mice. ${ }^{8}$ Conversely, the immune system influences the composition and quality of the gut microbiota. ${ }^{9}$ Accordingly, IBD development is associated with an altered gut microbiota, ${ }^{10}$ but it is uncertain whether microbiota imbalances are the result or cause of gastrointestinal inflammation, especially in CGD. The impact of ROS production in phagocyte subsets on intestinal homeostasis remains an additional puzzle.Hence, we explored genetically distinct CGD models with spontaneous and induced IBD by multidimensional analysis. We found that NOX2 deficiency was linked to impaired monocyte differentiation and early gut microbiota development. Introduction of complex microbiota into CGD mice substantially improved preclinical modeling, which may eventually facilitate translational progress for a life-threatening immune-mediated disease.

## Methods

## Mice

All used mouse strains were on C57BL/6J or 6 N background. B6.129S-Cybbtm1Din/J (gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox-/- }}$ ) and B6N.129S2Ncf1tm1Shl/J ( $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ ), as well as gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox-flox/flox }}$ and CD45.1, mice were from the Jackson Laboratory, Bar Harbor, ME. NSG.Cybb ${ }^{-/-}$mice, also known as X-CGD NSG, are a strain of NOD.scid.II2Rycnull (NSG) mice that also carry the Cybbem1Hmal knockout allele. NSG and X-CGD NSG mice were from The Jackson Laboratory. Cx3cr1 ${ }^{\text {cre/+ }}$ mice were provided by Marco Prinz (Freiburg, Germany). Mice were bred under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions at the Center for Experimental Models and Transgenic Service animal facilities (University of Freiburg). All animal experiments were conducted with approval from the Federal Ministry for Nature, Environment, and Consumer Protection of the state of BadenWuerttemberg (identifier G16/147, G21/028, and G22/104).

## Colitis models

For dextran sodium sulfate (DSS) colitis, male mice were administered $1.5 \%$ (wt/vol) DSS in sterile drinking water for 7 days, followed by 1 day of normal drinking water. For trinitrobenzene sulfonic acid (TNBS) colitis, male mice were presensitized by topical application of TNBS $1 \%$ (wt/vol) in $\mathrm{H}_{2} \mathrm{O}$ on shaved dorsal skin. Afterward, $2.5 \%$ TNBS was administered intrarectally. ${ }^{11}$

## Animal interventions

For crossfostering, $50 \%$ of litters were exchanged between dams at postnatal day 1 (P1) to P3. B6 wildlings were created through inverse germ-free derivation. ${ }^{12}$ Wildlings were kept under seminatural housing conditions through cage supplementation with natural materials, including hay, compost, and fomites from wild mice. For infections with Corynebacterium kutscheri, $10^{8}$ colony-forming units (CFUs) were orally gavaged to 2-week-old mice. For bone marrow chimera, mice were irradiated with $2 \times 4.5 \mathrm{~Gy}$, followed by IV injection of $5 \times 10^{6}$ bone marrow cells.

## Patients with CGD and healthy donors

Biopsies were obtained from 5 healthy donors and 3 patients with CGD with gastrointestinal symptoms undergoing diagnostic colonoscopy at the Freiburg University Medical Center (patients with CGD, $n=3$; including 2 patients with $N C F 1^{-/-}$ and 1 patient without a defined mutation).

## Lamina propria cell isolation

Cells were isolated as described previously. ${ }^{13}$ Briefly, the colon was cut and incubated for 30 minutes in dissociation buffer at $37^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, followed by digestion with $0.3 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{mL}$ collagenase IV, 5 $\mathrm{U} / \mathrm{mL}$ dispase, and $0.5 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{mL}$ DNase I.

## Statistical analysis

Unpaired $t$ tests were used to compare 2 groups, and for multiple groups, 1-way or 2-way analysis of variance with Tukey multiple comparisons test was used, if not stated otherwise. Sample size ( $n$ ) denotes biological replicates, and data points are presented as mean $\pm$ standard error of the mean of independent experiments, as indicated. Differences were considered significant at ${ }^{*} P<.05,{ }^{* *} P<.01$, and ${ }^{* * *} P<.001$.

Detailed methods can be found in the supplemental Methods, available on the Blood website.

## Results

## NOX2-deficient mice exhibit low-grade intestinal inflammation

In contrast to humans with CGD, NOX2-deficient mice do not spontaneously develop overt colitis. ${ }^{1,14}$ To explore the baseline tissue reaction, we profiled the immune cell composition in the colon in gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice (Cybb ${ }^{-/-}$) and $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice (Ncf1 ${ }^{-/-}$). In the gut, Ly6C ${ }^{+}$MHC-II ${ }^{-}$monocytes continuously infiltrate the lamina propria, where they form Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-\mathrm{II}^{+}$tissuesresident macrophages (supplemental Figure 1A ${ }^{15}$ ). Compared with wild-type (WT) mice, both $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice showed a significant increase in intestinal Ly6C ${ }^{+}$MHC-II ${ }^{-}$monocytes and a reduction in mature Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-\mathrm{II}^{+}$macrophages (Figure 1A-B). Similarly, NSG mice, which lack T and B cells, showed a disturbed monocyte-macrophage waterfall when Cybb was mutated, suggesting that the alteration was independent of adaptive immunity (supplemental Figure 1B). Notably, the complex alterations beyond direct macrophage-lymphocyte interaction in NSG mice may limit the interpretation of our findings, ie, it does not exclude that adaptive immune cells have an impact on intestinal macrophage differentiation in CGD. Histological examinations revealed hypertrophy in the muscularis externa and mucosa, as well as crypt structure alterations (supplemental Figure 1C). Furthermore, neutrophils infiltrated the lamina propria (Figure 1C; supplemental Figure 1D). A combination of increased monocyte influx and reduced presence of tissuesresident macrophages was observed in NOX2-deficient mice at different ages (supplemental Figure 1E). CD68 ${ }^{+}$macrophage accumulation at the crypt bases and tops in aged NOX2-deficient mice (supplemental Figure 1F), crypt structure alterations, and goblet cell reduction (supplemental Figure 1G) indicated intestinal inflammation. ${ }^{16}$ In contrast, the small-intestine and the skin were not altered (supplemental Figure 1H-I). In addition, NOX2 deficiency did not affect macrophage CSF-driven differentiation of bone marrow progenitors into macrophages in vitro (supplemental Figure 1J-L), indicating that ROS absence did not intrinsically affect macrophage differentiation.

Gene ontology enrichment analysis of upregulated genes in NOX2-deficient mice (Figure 1F), revealed that NOX2 deficiency affected chronic inflammatory responses and granulocyte and mononuclear cell migration (Figure 1G). ### Heightened susceptibility to colitis and impaired monocyte differentiation in p47<sup>phox-/-</sup> mice

Although NOX2 deficiency disturbed myeloid cell homeostasis in tissue, bone marrow, and blood, we did not observe clinical signs of colitis. Hence, we next explored the well-established DSS colitis model.20,21 Administration of 1.5% DSS in the drinking water for 7 days resulted in a modest reduction in body weight and mild colitis in WT mice (Figure 2A-D), whereas p47<sup>phox-/-</sup> mice showed excessive weight loss, requiring termination of the experiment by day 8 (Figure 2A). The colon length was strongly reduced (Figure 2B), and mice exhibited bloody diarrhea and a high disease activity index (Figure 2C-E). The antimicrobial peptide Reg3y22,23 and IL1b were transcriptionally increased in the colon of p47<sup>phox-/-</sup> mice (Figure 2F). In contrast, Cx3cl1, which regulates the development of subepithelial macrophages,19 was downregulated (Figure 2F). P47<sup>phox-/-</sup> mice exhibited pronounced inflammation in the distal colon, including loss of goblet cells and crypt structure (Figure 2G). Moreover, p47<sup>phox-/-</sup> mice displayed an increase of Ly6C<sup>high</sup>-monocytes and a concurrent decline in tissue-resident MHC-II<sup>+</sup> macrophages (Figures 2H-I). This was in contrast to the situation in WT mice with DSS, which showed increased fueling of the monocyte-to-macrophage waterfall with higher numbers of Ly6C<sup>+</sup>MHC-II<sup>+</sup> cells (Figure 2I), as described before.6,17,24 Notably, p47<sup>phox-/-</sup> mice also displayed double-negative Ly6C<sup>+</sup>MHC-II<sup>+</sup>CD64<sup>+</sup> macrophages (Figure 2I), which have been described previously to occur in the small intestine under inflammation.25 Moreover, neutrophils were increased, whereas eosinophils were decreased in the colon (Figure 2J). Neutrophils were also substantially increased in the peripheral blood, whereas Ly6C<sup>low</sup> and Ly6C<sup>int</sup> monocytes were decreased in p47<sup>phox-/-</sup> mice (Figure 2K).

Additionally, the number of cells expressing neither CCR2 nor HLA-DR was increased (Figure 2M), similar to our observations in p47<sup>phox</sup>-deficient mice. ## Colitis affects hematopoiesis in the bone marrow

Next, we explored the impact of colitis on myelopoiesis in the bone marrow (Figure 3A ${ }^{27,28}$ ). We found an increase in common myeloid progenitors (CMPs) and granulocyte-macrophage progenitors in WT mice after DSS treatment (Figure 3B-C). However, in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice with and without DSS early progenitors, ie, the CMPs, were reduced (Figure 3C). We hypothesized that this may result from accelerated differentiation into progeny. Although multipotent granulocyte-macrophage progenitors (Figure 3C) and inducible-monocyte progenitors (iMoPs) were similar to WT mice (Figure 3D-E), monocyte-dendritic cell progenitors and common monocyte progenitors were reduced after DSS treatment (Figure 3D-E), and Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high }}$ monocytes and granulocytes were elevated in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ bone marrow in colitis (Figure 3E). This suggested accelerated cell differentiation downstream of CMPs, potentially driven by increased effector cell demands in the periphery (Figures 1 and 2). In agreement, Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high}}$-monocytes were increased in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice, whereas Ly6C ${ }^{\text {low }}$ monocytes, the presumed progeny of Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high }}$ monocytes ${ }^{29}$ (Figure 3A), were significantly reduced (Figure 3E). Thus, Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high }}$ monocytes might have lacked the time to differentiate into Ly6C ${ }^{\text {low }}$ monocytes in the bone marrow. This phenomenon was also reflected by reduced Ly6C ${ }^{\text {low }}$ monocytes in peripheral blood (Figure 2K). We hypothesized that the heightened demand for monocytes triggered emergency monocytopoiesis, ${ }^{30}$ causing a skewed differentiation of bone marrow progenitors during colitis. Transcriptional analysis of sorted progenitors from DSS-treated $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice showed downregulation of Csf1r, Ccr2, MyD88, Cd74, and Msr1, accompanied by upregulation of Ms4a3 (Figure 3F), which was indicative of a more immature cell state. Collectively, these data demonstrate that the absence of $p 47^{\text {phox }}$ results in substantial disturbances in myelopoiesis during colitis.

## Bone marrow transplantation partially rescues colitis in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice

Allogenic hematopoietic cell transplantation cures CGD, including its immunopathological symptoms. ${ }^{31}$ However, a previous study found that bone marrow transplantation did not rescue colitis in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice. ${ }^{14}$ In view of the profound alterations in the myeloid compartment in our CGD colitis model, we revisited this issue. We transplanted both $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and WT mice with either $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ or WT bone marrow and induced DSS colitis 8 weeks later (supplemental Figure 2A). Although $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }} \rightarrow p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ chimeras had a similar phenotype as $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice without transplantation (Figure 2A), WT $\rightarrow p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ chimeras displayed reduced susceptibility to DSS colitis in terms of weight loss and colon length (supplemental Figure 2B-C), reduced neutrophils in the colon compared with $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }} \rightarrow p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ chimeras (supplemental Figure 2D), and improved histological parameters, in particular crypt architecture (supplemental Figure 2E). On the other hand, $P 47^{\text {phox-/- }} \rightarrow \mathrm{WT}$ chimeras showed a stronger susceptibility to colitis (supplemental Figure 2B-E). Moreover, WT $\rightarrow p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ chimeras exhibited a normal monocyte-to-macrophage waterfall, ultimately giving rise to Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-\mathrm{II}^{+}$resident macrophages
(supplemental Figure 2F). On the contrary, Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-\mathrm{II}^{+}$tissuesresident macrophages were reduced in WT mice, in which monocytes lacked $p 47^{\text {phox }} \quad\left(p 47^{\text {phox-/- }} \rightarrow\right.$ WT [supplemental Figure 2F]). Hence, it appeared that the ability by phagocytes to produce ROS was a prerequisite for monocytes entering the intestinal wall to differentiate into mature and potentially anergic macrophages. These data underlined that cells derived from definitive hematopoiesis contributed to CGD colitis development. Accordingly, reinstating ROS in bone marrow-derived cells reduced susceptibility to intestinal inflammation, although not to the same level as observed in WT mice.

## Reduced susceptibility to colitis in gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox }}$ deficient mice is linked to microbiota

Mutations in CYBB represent the most common form of CGD in humans and are often associated with colitis. However, when we subjected $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice to DSS treatment, body weight loss (Figure 4A), colon length (Figure 4B), histology (Figure 4C), and monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation (Figure 4D) were comparable with WT mice, with even lower neutrophil recruitment to the colon (supplemental Figure 3A). Thus, although $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice showed similar baseline disturbances in lamina propria macrophage differentiation (Figure 1A-B), they responded very differently to DSS.

To explore whether differences between NOX2 variant strains were specific to the DSS model, we additionally analyzed the TNBS model, which causes colitis via a T-lymphocyte response (Figure 4E). ${ }^{11}$ TNBS administration resulted in increased weight loss in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice compared with $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice, which were again similar to WT controls (Figure 4E). Moreover, colon shortening, mucosal damage, and goblet cell loss were more pronounced in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice than in $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice (Figure 4F-G). In $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice, submucosal edemas were also observed, but crypt structure and goblet cells remained intact (Figure 4G). Microscopy and flow cytometry of colon tissue showed reduced macrophages after TNBS administration in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice (Figure 4H-I). Although both genotypes displayed increased monocytes (Figure 4I) and neutrophils (supplemental Figure 3B), as well as decreased numbers of mature Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-\mathrm{II}^{+}$macrophages compared to WT mice in the colon, $g p 91^{\text {phox }}$-deficient mice had significantly more Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-\mathrm{II}^{+}$cells than $p 47^{\text {phox }}$-deficient mice (Figure 4H-I). Next, we analyzed whether transcriptional profile in resident colonic macrophages discriminated $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice without colitis induction, observing notable transcriptional differences between the CGD strains (Figure 4J).

Because both NOX2 models exhibited similar homeostatic alterations in macrophage differentiation and ROS formation, and because transplantation of WT bone marrow did not fully revert the induced CGD colitis phenotype, we wondered whether nongenetic causes accounted for the differences between the 2 mouse strains. ## Early-life microbiota development affects colitis susceptibility in CGD

To further investigate the role of the intestinal microbiota, we performed crossfostering, guided by the hypothesis that the maternal microbiota imprinted the microbiota development of the pups. Accordingly, neonatal $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice were swapped between dams between P1 and P3, and DSS colitis was induced at age 6 to 8 weeks (Figure 5A). Crossfostered $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice raised by $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ dams displayed somewhat reduced weight loss compared with $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice raised by isogenic dams (Figure 5B), despite a similar shortening in colon length (Figure 5C). Accordingly, infant crossfostered $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice exhibited a modest increase in mucosal cell infiltration, partial loss of crypt architecture, and submucosal edema when raised by $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ dams (Figure 5D). Furthermore, crossfostering partially altered other inflammatory parameters, such as the number of blood neutrophils and abundance of Ly6C ${ }^{+}$MHC-II ${ }^{-}$and Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-$ II $^{+}$colonic macrophages (Figure 5E-G). Yet, despite very early crossfostering, $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice remained significantly different in their response to DSS with respect to weight loss and colon length (Figure 5B-C). Together, the data indicated that crossfostering resulted in convergence of the colitis phenotypes, however, the effects were overall rather modest and in part variable.

We next explored how crossfostering affected the microbiota and analyzed fecal samples by 16 S ribosomal RNA gene sequencing. Surprisingly, $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice maintained lower microbiota diversity, despite crossfostering (Figure 5H), whereas crossfostered $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice also displayed a lower richness of bacterial species in feces (Figure 5H). However, beta diversity analysis indicated that the mouse genotypes exhibited similar phylogenetic profiles of their microbiota, regardless of
whether they were raised by their mother or a genetically distinct foster mother (Figure 5I). This suggested that the microbiota profile was already established very early in life and could not be effectively modified by crossfostering. In combination with the results outlined above, these findings indicated that a microbiota seeded in the first 24 to 72 hours of life severely affects the induced CGD colitis phenotype.

## "Wildling" microbiota mediates colitis and granuloma formation in CGD mice

The microbiome affects macrophage renewal rates under both healthy and inflammatory conditions. ${ }^{41}$ Recent studies have shown that mice with a highly complex microbiome from wild mice (wildling) may more closely resemble the human immune response and be superior in disease modeling. ${ }^{12,42}$ Accordingly, we assessed the effect of wildling microbiota on intestinal macrophages and CGD colitis. We crossfostered CGD mice at P1 to P3 to wildling dams (Figure 6A). Interestingly, wildling WT mice showed much higher numbers of Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high }}$ monocytes in the colon at P14 than SPF mice (supplemental Figure 4A). The Ly6C ${ }^{-} \mathrm{MHC}-\mathrm{II}^{-}$macrophage population, indicative of prenatal tissue-resident macrophages, was absent in wildling mice (supplemental Figure $4 A^{15,43,44}$ ). This suggested rapid turnover of prenatal macrophages in wildling mice already before weaning. Moreover, MHC-II ${ }^{+}$tissue-resident macrophages expressed less $\mathrm{CX}_{3} \mathrm{CR1}$ (supplemental Figure 4B), and neutrophils were increased in the colon of wildling mice, whereas eosinophils were diminished (supplemental Figure 4C). Hence, the wildling microbiota was associated with accelerated intestinal phagocyte maturation and increased myeloid cell infiltration.
$P 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice crossfostered to wildling dams exhibited a largely similar severe clinical phenotype, which was strikingly different to that of WT mice. Both CGD strains showed decreased survival rates (Figure 6B) and reduced weight gain (Figure 6C) and colon length (Figure 6D). Notably, C kutscheri was cultured from the liver and spleen of several surviving NOX2-deficient mice yet was rarely found in WT mice (Figure 6E). C kutscheri is a regular gut inhabitant and mouse pathogen not present in SPF facilities. Accordingly, the wildling CGD phenotype was associated with much increased translocation of a potentially virulent bacterium and sepsis. In agreement with this, neutrophils and Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high }}$ monocytes were elevated in the peripheral blood (supplemental Figure 4D).

## Macrophage-derived ROS contributes to intestinal homeostasis

Next, we exploited the robust disease phenotype in wildling CGD mice to explore selective NOX2 deficiency in tissue macrophages, given their disturbed development across all models. Because $\mathrm{CX}_{3} \mathrm{CR1}$ is highly expressed in lamina propria macrophages but not in neutrophils, ${ }^{24,45}$ we generated Cx3cr1 ${ }^{\text {cre/+; }}$ : $g p 91^{\text {phox-flox/flox }}$ mice ${ }^{46}$ and crossfostered the offspring to wildling mice (Figure 6O). In Cx3cr1 ${ }^{\text {cre/+; }}$ :gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox-flox/flox }}$ mice, ROS was absent in Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high }}$ monocytes but was normal in neutrophils (supplemental Figure 5A-B). In addition, blood monocytes of Cx3cr1 ${ }^{\text {cre/+; }}$ :gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox-flox/flox }}$ showed a significant reduction in Cybb gene expression (supplemental Figure 5C). In line with a critical role of macrophages in CGD, Cx3cr1 ${ }^{\text {cre/+; }}$ :gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox-flox/flox }}$ mice exhibited a lower survival rate and increased diarrhea compared with Cre ${ }^{-}$mice (Figure 6P-Q). Although both groups were similar in weight gain (supplemental Figure 5D), neutrophils (supplemental Figure 5E-F) and Ly6C ${ }^{\text {high }}$ monocytes were increased in bone marrow and colon (supplemental Figure 5G-H), and colonic resident macrophages were slightly decreased (supplemental Figure 5H). In histology, Cx3cr1 ${ }^{\text {cre/+; }}$ :gp91 ${ }^{\text {phox-flox/flox }}$ mice showed inflammation and loss of goblet cells in the colon (Figure 6R) and spontaneous granuloma formation in the liver (Figure 6S). Hence, the loss of functional NOX2 in monocytes/ macrophages was sufficient to cause intestinal inflammation in the context of a complex microbiota.

## Discussion

Here, we link the lack of ROS with dysregulation in monocyte-to-macrophage differentiation, ranging from myeloid progenitor cells to fully differentiated lamina propria macrophages in 2 distinct lines of NOX2 deficiency, although these strains did not show clinical signs of colitis under SPF conditions. Yet, if exposed to a complex microbiota immediately after birth, both strains developed clinical CGD hallmarks: spontaneous colitis, sepsis with mucosal commensals, and liver granulomas. Improving mouse CGD models by combining them with complex microbiota was a prerequisite for uncovering the specific contribution of tissue macrophages to the inflammatory complications of the disease. Moreover, analysis of colon biopsies
from patients supported the involvement of macrophage differentiation in CGD-IBD.

Deficiency in $g p 91^{\text {phox }}$ is the predominant and most severe genetic cause of CGD in patients, in whom an early onset of the disease phenotype leads to premature death. ${ }^{47}$ Nevertheless, it remained a mystery in the field why $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice were relatively resistant to induced colitis, in contrast to highly susceptible $p 47^{\text {phox }}$-deficient mice. ${ }^{14,48-50}$ To gain new ground, we analyzed the cellular innate gut immunity in both mouse strains site by site and found substantial alterations in macrophage differentiation in the circulation and intestinal tissue. However, the divergent phenotypes of induced colitis in the 2 mouse models under SPF conditions posed an obstacle to understanding the role of ROS in the pathogenesis of CGD-IBD. ${ }^{48-50}$

CGD mice with wildling microbiota spontaneously developed a severe postnatal inflammatory phenotype, irrespective of the exact genetic cause. The microbiota signature converged in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ and $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ wildling mice, whereas the SPF environment fixed a microbiota in $g p 91^{\text {phox }}$-deficient mice that corrupted the clinical phenotype. Additionally, we observed that, in $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice, lower alpha diversity was associated with less severe colitis. This may seem counterintuitive, because lower bacterial alpha diversity is often found under pathological conditions in humans, including patients with CGD-IBD. ${ }^{51,52}$ However, a lower alpha diversity was not observed in $p 22^{\text {phox-/- }}\left[C Y B A^{-/-}\right]$ patients. This variability highlights the ambiguous role of microbiome diversity in intestinal inflammation and immunodeficiency. ${ }^{52}$ Taken together, it seems difficult to transfer alpha diversity results obtained in laboratory mice, which are maintained under strict hygienic conditions, to humans. It is tempting to speculate that the lower diversity in $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice either reflected the loss of detrimental taxa that contributed to disease development in $p 47^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice or that compensatory mechanisms in $g p 91^{\text {phox-/- }}$ mice mitigated the impact of reduced alpha diversity on colitis severity.

The combination of the CGD mice with wildling microbiota constitutes a substantial step forward in modeling the inflammatory phenotype of an important inborn error of immunity. The data presented here are in line with the discovery that increasing the complexity of the microbiota beyond "regular" SPF conditions may help resolving the pervasive "prediction problem" in translational research; that is, the limited transferability of a significant portion of findings generated using laboratory rodents. ${ }^{53}$

Clinical data support that allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation is the only curative option for patients with CGD leading to a significant increase in survival. However, previous studies failed to confirm a beneficial effect of bone marrow transplantation in mice. ${ }^{14}$ In contrast, our refined model showed that bone marrow transplantation significantly improved the clinical phenotype in mice subjected to DSS treatment. Yet bone marrow transplantation did not completely resolve colitis in CGD mice, suggesting that phagocyte ROS deficiency has persistent effects on intestinal homeostasis. In accordance with our data on the interrelation of microbiota and ROS deficiency in CGD, it was previously found that only early modification of the microbiota has a significant impact on colitis outcome. ${ }^{14}$ However, in contrast to this study, which stated that the microbiome established at birth has more significant influence than ROS on colitis in $p 47^{p 50 a-/-}$ mice, we show that this conclusion cannot be drawn if a complex or more "natural" microbiome is present. Overall, our study shows that monocytes and macrophages play a critical role in the development of colitis in mouse models of CGD and are altered in human patients. Directions for future translational studies on CGD-IBD may include those exploring the monocyte/macrophage-specific role of cytokines induced by bacterial taxa altered in IBD as well as the impact of abnormal efferocytosis in CGD.

In conclusion, genetic factors, tissue macrophage development, and microbiota act in concert to cause pathology in CGD. This complexity leading to severe inflammation can only be untangled in preclinical models if all individual components are dissected with highest scrutiny.
