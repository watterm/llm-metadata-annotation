Flexible response mechanisms would seem most suitable to accommodate the dynamic challenges of effective antimicrobial defense and restoration of tissue homeostasis. Here, we dissected macrophage-intrinsic mechanisms and microenvironmental cues that tune macrophage signaling in localized skin infection with the colonizing and opportunistic pathogen Staphylococcus aureus. Early in skin infection, the cytokine granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF) produced by $\gamma \delta$ T cells and hypoxic conditions within the dermal microenvironment diverted macrophages away from a homeostatic M-CSF- and hypoxia-inducible factor 1a (HIF-1a)-dependent program. This allowed macrophages to be metabolically rewired for maximal inflammatory activity, which requires expression of Irg1 and generation of itaconate, but not HIF-1a. This multifactorial macrophage rewiring program was required for both the timely clearance of bacteria and for the provision of local immune memory. These findings indicate that immunometabolic conditioning allows dermal macrophages to cycle between antimicrobial activity and protection against secondary infections.

## INTRODUCTION

The skin hosts a dynamic microflora composed of fungi and bacteria, including Staphylococcus aureus, which colonizes mucocutaneous surfaces of the anterior nares and moist skin in up to $30 \%$ of humans, who are mostly asymptomatic (1). Colonization frequencies are particularly high at the beginning of life, when the individual microflora is established (2). Staphylococcal strains are usually largely unchanged in a host over long periods. Yet S. aureus has high pathogenic potential and as such is a leading cause of acute bacterial skin infections (3). Thus, a carefully balanced and highly flexible host-microbe interface is essential for coexistence. Children with cellular innate immunodeficiency, such as genetic aberration in Toll-like (TLR) and interleukin-1 (IL-1) receptor signaling (IRAK4/MyD88 deficiency), are exceedingly susceptible to $S$. aureus infections, underlining the importance of innate immunity

[^0]for infection control $(4,5)$. In line with this, tissue-resident macrophages (Macs), the most abundant immune cell type in the resting dermis, are key for controlling S. aureus skin infection (3, 6). Deficiencies in dermal Macs or MyD88 are similar with respect to impaired bacterial clearance and dysregulated neutrophil recruitment (7), and dermal Macs mediate transient innate immune memory with enhanced response to secondary S. aureus infection (8). Whereas a central role for dermal Macs in S. aureus defense has been established, the molecular and cellular dynamics, and functional hierarchies of heterocellular networks around Macs in skin infection, have not been resolved. In resting skin, dermal Macs consist of at least three definable subsets with discrete renewal patterns involving cells from primitive and definitive hematopoiesis (9-13). Macs continually survey and interact with the microenvironment and can undergo drastic changes during skin infection via coactivation and recruitment of circulating immune cells. Furthermore, the bacterial infection has high metabolic costs with altered energy and nutrient availability due to localized hypoxia, malperfusion (14), and nutrient competition between immune cells and invading pathogens $(15,16)$. Consequently, dermal Macs maintain high plasticity to respond to the variable microenvironment. The majority of tissue-resident Macs, including dermal Macs $(9,17)$, rely on macrophage colony-stimulating factor (MCSF) receptor signaling for their maintenance during homeostasis. Granulocyte-macrophage colony-stimulating factor (GM-CSF), on the other hand, is largely dispensable in steady state for most tissue Macs, with the notable exception of alveolar Macs $(18,19)$, but is relevant at sites of tissue inflammation (20) or bacterial infections (21-24). GM-CSF exerts immunomodulatory effects by boosting glycolysis in monocytes to drive cytokine production $(23,25)$, highlighting a link between GM-CSF in the microenvironment and alterations in carbohydrate metabolism. In contrast to thorough in vitro studies on Mac metabolism affecting immune functions, the link between dynamic cellular alterations during clearance of infection and reestablishment of tissue homeostasis remains largely elusive. Here, we show that dermal Macs engage a dynamic and multifaceted metabolic program during skin infection. In response to S. aureus and local hypoxia, homeostatic M-CSF-conditioned dermal Macs initially required HIF-1 $\alpha$ and generated energy through glycolysis. The switch to sustained glycolysis was driven by T cell-derived GM-CSF, which unleashed an alternative hypoxia Mac program that was HIF-1 $\alpha$ independent and contributed to immune memory acquisition. This program further involved the up-regulation of $\operatorname{Irg1}$ (immune responsive gene 1) and itaconate production, which were linked to the resolution of inflammation. Interference with any aspect of this stepwise cellular tuning delayed bacterial clearance and boosted tissue inflammation.

## RESULTS

## Dermal Macs undergo cellular reprogramming during $S$. aureus infection

In staphylococcal skin infection, the complex kinetic changes in Mac and granulocyte composition cannot be sufficiently explained by cell-intrinsic activation patterns and bacterial burden $(7,8)$. In wild-type (wt) mice, S. aureus intradermal (id) infection was selfresolving within 1 week (Fig. 1A). Concomitant with this, skin neutrophils and IL-1 $\beta$ tissue concentrations peaked at 1 day post-infection (dpi) and then waned until 7 dpi (Fig. 1B and fig. S1G). To comprehensively map dynamic changes across canonical immune cell types, we performed high parametric flow cytometry combined with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) (28) dimensionality reduction and FlowSOM meta-clustering (29) on extracted tissue cells from mice infected with S. aureus in both ears for 1, S, and 7 days. This approach revealed highly complex kinetic profiles of 17 immune cell populations in control and infected mice (fig. S1, A and B). At 7 dpi, CD4, CD8, and $\mathrm{CD} 4^{-} \mathrm{CD} 8^{-} \alpha \beta$ T cells in the dermis steadily increased in number, similarly to V $\gamma 6$ $\gamma \delta$ T cells, whereas V $\gamma 4 \gamma \delta$ T cells increased until 5 dpi before decreasing at 7 dpi (fig. S1C). In contrast, B cells, innate lymphoid cells (ILCs), and mast cells remained relatively constant in number (fig. S1D). By 5 to 7 dpi natural killer (NK) cells and eosinophils accumulated in the tissue (fig. S1E), as did dendritic cells (DCs), monocytes, and CD64 ${ }^{\text {Hi }}$ Macs (fig. S1F).

Dermal CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs, signaling via MyD88, are essential for bacterial clearance (7). To gain insight into the signaling pathways that mediate infection clearance beyond MyD88 $(7,8)$, we sorted CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ dermal Macs and performed RNA sequencing (RNAseq). Despite the self-limiting course of the infection (7), we found large changes in gene expression in the dermal Macs compartment with 4034, 4726, and 5993 differentially expressed genes on days 1,5 , and 7 , respectively, as compared with Macs from control mice (fig. S1H).

Next, we expanded our immunophenotyping beyond CD64 (used to identify dermal Macs in fig. S1A) by including CD206, ty-rosine-protein kinase MER (MERTK), and major histocompatibility complex class II (MHC-II). This approach revealed the
heterogeneity of dermal Macs because we identified five distinct clusters over the course of infection (Fig. 1, C to F). During homeostasis, CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs could be divided into CD206 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ and CD206 ${ }^{\text {Int }}$ subsets (Fig. 1C and fig. S1I) (12). In addition, cells coexpressing CD64 and Ly6C were present in the steady-state dermis (Fig. 1, C and D), suggesting a developmental pathway from infiltrating monocytes. This agreed with our previous data demonstrating that a large fraction of resident Macs are continuously renewed by tissue-homing Ly6C ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ monocytes, which up-regulate CD64, whereas a population of embryonically derived, nerve-associated Macs remains until adulthood $(7,8)$. At 1 dpi , monocyte-derived Macs infiltrated the dermis, as indicated by a notable increase in cells coexpressing CD64 and Ly6C (Fig. 1, C and D, and fig. S1I). At later time points, an increase in the Macs expressing MHC-II potentially reflected enhanced interactions with adaptive immune cells. We also observed the up-regulation of arginase 1 (ARG1)and nitric oxide synthase 2 (NOS2)-expressing Macs at 5 or 7 dpi, indicative of activation (Fig. 1, C and D, and fig. S1I).

We next determined whether prior resident and newly recruited Macs differed in their inflammatory response to staphylococci and whether differences in marker expression at the Mac population level (such as MHC-II, NOS2, or ARG1) resulted from preferential expression by specific subsets with distinct origins. Thus, we examined Cxcr4 ${ }^{\text {CreERT2/+: }} R 26^{\text {CAG-LSL-tdT }}$ mice, where HSC-derived monocytes (but not CD206 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs) are labeled by induction with tamoxifen (TAM) (fig. S1J, top) (30). We induced with TAM 1 week before infection and could distinguish Macs already present in the tissue (Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Lo}}$ ) and those newly recruited (Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ ). At steady state, $\sim 15 \%$ of CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs were Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$, which increased to $\sim 50 \%$ at 1 dpi (Fig. 1, G and H). We observed increased Il1b expression in both Mac subsets (Fig. 1I), albeit with higher expression in the newly recruited cells. Accordingly, we observed pro-IL-1 $\beta$ expression, analyzed by intracellular staining at 1 dpi , in both CD206 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ and Ly6C ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ subsets, although the response was more robust within Ly6C ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs (Fig. 1, J and K). In addition, in Cxcr4 ${ }^{\text {CreERT2/+: }} R 26^{\text {CAG-LSL-tdT }}$ mice, both NOS2 and ARG1 were increased in Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs on 5 and 7 dpi (Fig. 1L and fig. S1K).

To complement these data, we analyzed Mrc1 ${ }^{\text {CreERT2/CreERT2 }}$ :R26 ${ }^{\text {CAG-LSL-tdT }}$ mice (31). In these mice, TAM induction labels CD206-expressing cells. About $85 \%$ of the CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs expressed Tomato 1 week after TAM induction in steady state (Fig. 1M), whereas Ly6C ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs remained unlabeled (fig. S1J, lower). After infection, the frequency of Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ cells decreased (Fig. 1N). Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs (i.e., those present before infection) also up-regulated NOS2 and ARG1 at 5 and 7 dpi (Fig. 1O and fig. S1L). Similarly, at 5 dpi, both Tomato-positive (prior resident) and -negative (newly recruited) Macs showed increased MHC-II expression (Fig. 1, P and Q). These data indicated that a self-limiting $S$. aureus infection induces dynamic changes in the immune cell composition of the skin and that both prior resident and newly recruited Macs robustly respond during infection.

To determine whether the local provision of IFN- $\beta$ affected infection resolution, we generated LysM ${ }^{\text {Cis }}$ :Ifnar ${ }^{\beta / \beta}$ mice (referred to as Ifnar ${ }^{-/-}$) in which type I IFN signaling is disrupted in most tissue-resident phagocytes. At 5 dpi , we observed increased bacterial burdens in Ifnar ${ }^{-/-}$mice compared with wt controls (fig. S2C). Transcriptome analysis also revealed increased expression of genes associated with the positive regulation of GM-CSF (indicated by an arrow on Fig. 2A) and genes involved in downstream pathways, such as the Janus kinase-signal transducers and activators of transcription (JAK-STAT) signaling cascade $(32,33)$ (Fig. 2A). In agreement, GM-CSF was detected in wholetissue lysates after infection and was maintained at consistent levels until 7 dpi (Fig. 2B), implicating GM-CSF as a basal factor in $S$. aureus-induced inflammation. The presence of GM-CSF in the tissue was associated with decreased expression of Csf1r (encoding the M-CSF receptor) and increased expression of Csf2rb (encoding the signaling subunit of the GM-CSF receptor) (Fig. 2C) and Jak2 (fig. S2D). The decreased expression of Csf1r was also observed in CCR2-deficient mice (at 1, 5, and 7 dpi ), indicating that its reduction was not due to monocyte influx (fig. S2E). Increased expression of Csf2rb was maintained for up to 3 weeks after infection (fig. S2F). Altered CSF1 and CSF2 receptor expression was confirmed at 1 dpi (Fig. 2, D and E, and fig. S2G). Collectively, these results indicated that, as early as 1 dpi , dermal Macs adopt a transcriptional response that is GM-CSF dependent at the expense of the homeostatic MCSF program.

To gain further insight into the impact of GM-CSF-induced programming, we analyzed the 25 most up-regulated genes expressed by Macs at 1 dpi . We then examined the expression of the identified genes in an RNA-seq dataset generated in bone marrowderived Macs (BMDMs) that were cultured in either M-CSF or GMCSF and stimulated with heat-fixed (hf) S. aureus. The majority of the top 25 up-regulated genes in Macs at 1 dpi were expressed by both M-CSF and GM-CSF BMDMs upon S. aureus stimulation. However, 21 of 25 genes were expressed to a significantly higher degree in BMDMs cultured in GM-CSF and stimulated with $S$. aureus as compared with stimulated M-CSF BMDMs. Nineteen of 25 of these genes were already up-regulated in the GM-CSF control group as compared with M-CSF controls (Fig. 2F). A similar trend was observed on days 5 and 7 after infection (fig. S2, H and I).

To confirm that the transcriptional changes observed in vitro also manifested in vivo, CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs were sorted from wt and Csf2rb-deficient mice at 1 dpi and analyzed with quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR). Macs from Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$mice had reduced expression of the GM-CSF "target genes" Slfn4, Il12a, Il23a, and S100a9 as well as the inflammatory genes Il1b and Il6 (Fig. 2G), indicating an overall impaired inflammatory activation in the absence of GM-CSF signaling.

At 1 dpi, two principal subgroups, distinguished by CD206 and Ly6C expression, were present in the skin. Thus, we determined whether GM-CSF was acting preferentially on Macs already in the tissue $\left(\mathrm{CD} 206^{\mathrm{Hi}}\right)$ or on cells newly recruited during infection $\left(\mathrm{Ly} 6 \mathrm{C}^{\mathrm{Hi}}\right)$. We sorted Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ and Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Lo}}$ Macs at 1 dpi from Cxcr4 ${ }^{\text {CreERT2/+: }} R 26^{\text {CAG-LSL-tdT }}$ mice (as in Fig. 1G) and found decreased expression of Csf1r in both resident and newly recruited Macs sorted from infected mice, whereas the expression of Csf2rb increased primarily in the Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ population (fig. S2J). In line
with this, expression of Slfn4, S100a9, and Ass1 was up-regulated in both groups, but to a higher degree in newly recruited cells (fig. S2K). In contrast, Il6, Il12a, and Il23a were exclusively expressed by Tomato ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs (fig. S2L). Next, we sorted CD206 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ and Ly6C ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs (Fig. 2H) from the skin of wt and Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$ mice at 1 dpi to dissect the GM-CSF impact on newly recruited and already differentiated tissue Macs. We found that Il1b expression was reduced in both Mac subsets in Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$mice as compared with wt mice (Fig. 2I). This suggested that GM-CSF was acting on both resident and newly recruited Macs but that the latter responded more robustly to infection. Thus, we determined in Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$mice whether GM-CSF-induced Mac activation was required for infection resolution. At 5 dpi, Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$mice had increased skin neutrophils (PML, polymorphonuclear leukocytes) compared with wt mice (Fig. 2J). In contrast, both groups did not differ in CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs (Fig. 2K) and CD206 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ and Ly6C ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ subsets (fig. S2M). Despite similar Mac frequencies, Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$mice had a higher bacterial burden as compared with wt controls (Fig. 2L) and increased levels of tissue IL-1 $\beta$, indicating prolonged inflammation (Fig. 2M). Increased PML numbers in Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$mice did not compensate for CSF2Rb deficiency, which is in line with our previous observation that the PML count later during dermal S. aureus infection is an indicator of a poor infection outcome and increased bacterial burden $(7,8)$.

To specifically target Csf2rb in dermal Macs, we crossed Mrc1 ${ }^{\text {CreERT2/CreERT2 }}: R 26^{\text {CAG-LSL-tdT }}(31)$ with Csf2rb ${ }^{\beta / \beta}$ mice to generate Mrc1 ${ }^{\text {CreERT2/+: }} \cdot \operatorname{Csf} 2 r b^{\beta / \beta}$ mice. Here, TAM induction allowed us to disrupt Csf2rb in CD206 ${ }^{+}$Macs without targeting newly recruited monocyte-derived Macs. After induction, these mice were intradermally infected and analyzed for bacterial burden at 5 dpi . As compared with Cre ${ }^{-}$control animals, Cre ${ }^{+}$mice had higher bacterial loads (Fig. 2N). The loss of antibacterial activity was similar to that observed in full Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$knockout (ko) mice at 5 dpi (Fig. 2L). Lastly, to complement data on recently HSC-derived versus a priori resident skin Macs in staphylococcal infection, we analyzed bone marrow (BM) chimeras, where wt or Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$BM was transferred to wt and Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$recipients. Ears were shielded during irradiation to protect resident dermal Macs. Following intradermal infection 8 weeks after transplantation, both wt-to-Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$and Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$-towt groups had higher bacterial loads as compared with the wt-to-wt control group (5 dpi; Fig. 2O). wt mice that received BM from Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$donors trended toward a higher bacterial burden than Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$mice that received wt BM. Overall, these results indicated that GM-CSF signaling was essential for bacterial clearance and inflammation resolution by reprogramming both prior resident and monocyte-derived Macs.

In contrast, at 1 dpi , these cells accumulated at the site of infection and appeared to localize in close spatial proximity to CD206 ${ }^{+}$Macs (Fig. 3, E to G, and fig. S3, H and I).

Next, we explored whether a particular subset of CD4 T cells predominantly produced GM-CSF at 7 dpi , considering the previously reported role of T helper $17\left(\mathrm{~T}_{\mathrm{H}} 17\right)$ cells in GM-CSF formation (36). Analysis of a limited set of cytokines revealed that the majority of CD4 T cells only expressed GM-CSF (approximately 44\%) at 7 dpi. However, some GM-CSF-positive T cells coexpressed IFN- $\gamma$ and tumor necrosis factor (TNF), whereas others coexpressed IL-17. Last, we observed minimal expression of IL-2, IL-10, IL-4, and IL22 at this time point. This suggested that the CD4 T cells present in the skin at 7 dpi contained both $\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{H}} 1$ and $\mathrm{T}_{\mathrm{H}} 17$ subsets (fig. S3, J and K).

We hypothesized that if $\gamma \delta \mathrm{T}$ cells were the initial source of GMCSF, their absence would result in delayed bacterial clearance (similarly to $\mathrm{Csf} 2 r b^{-/-}$mice). Dermal Macs sorted from $\gamma \delta T C R^{-/-}$mice (in which $\gamma \delta \mathrm{T}$ cells are not present; fig. S3L) at 1 dpi had reduced expression of Il1b, Il12a, Il23a, and S100a9 compared with wt controls (Fig. 3H). This was in line with reduced GM-CSF in wholetissue lysates (fig. S3M). Moreover, whereas $\gamma \delta T C R^{-/-}$mice did not harbor a significant alteration in Mac frequency (Fig. 3I and fig. S3N), these mice had increased skin neutrophils (Fig. 3J) and bacterial loads (Fig. 3K) at 5 dpi. However, note that the absence of $\gamma \delta \mathrm{T}$ cells may affect skin barrier re-epithelialization after injury. This could, in addition to disrupted GM-CSF production, contribute to the increased inflammation observed in these animals after infection.

Because dermal Macs maintained the expression of $\operatorname{Csf} 2 r b$ for up to 3 weeks after infection (fig. S2F), we tested whether GM-CSF was still produced at this time point and found GM-CSF ${ }^{+}$CD4 T cells (Fig. 3L). As mentioned above, S. aureus skin infection results in the acquisition of innate immune memory, an effect that is mediated by dermal Macs (8). However, the precise factors that contribute to memory acquisition in individual Macs remain elusive. We hypothesized that prolonged GM-CSF production may contribute to memory acquisition. Thus, we infected wt and $\operatorname{Csf} 2 r b^{-/-}$mice (primed) and waited 3 weeks before reinfecting them, along with mice infected for the first time (naïve) (Fig. 3M). Whereas primed wt mice exhibited enhanced bacterial killing, no difference was observed between primed and naïve $\operatorname{Csf} 2 r b^{-/-}$mice (Fig. 3N). To explore, which, if any, T cell subset was the primary source of GM-CSF in primed wt mice, we analyzed the dynamics of $\mathrm{CD}^{+}$ populations in reinfected mice. We found significant enrichment of several T cell subsets in primed mice as compared with uninfected controls. In particular, higher numbers of $\mathrm{V} \gamma 6, \mathrm{CD} 4$, and double-negative T cells were present in the skin of primed as compared with naïve mice at 5 dpi (fig. S3, O and P). Finally, intracellular GM-CSF staining revealed multiple cellular sources of GMCSF, with $\mathrm{V} \gamma 6 \mathrm{~T}$ cells and CD4 T cells producing higher levels in
primed mice as compared with their naïve counterparts at 5 dpi (fig. S3Q).

Together, these data indicate that several cell types contribute to GM-CSF production in the skin. Infiltrating $\gamma \delta \mathrm{T}$ cells are immediate suppliers directly after infection (i.e., at 1 dpi ), whereas at later time points, GM-CSF from CD4 and V $\gamma 6 \mathrm{~T}$ cells contributes to the acquisition of innate immune memory in dermal Macs.

## S. aureus and local hypoxia combine to promote glycolysis in dermal Macs

Regions of local hypoxia have been described at sites of bacterial infection (14). In agreement, our transcriptome analysis revealed upregulation of genes associated with a response to hypoxia (Fig. 2A). Hypoxic conditions result in changes in numerous pathways, including increased glycolysis to meet cellular adenosine $5^{\prime}$-triphosphate (ATP) requirements. The situation in Macs is more complex, because they alter their core metabolic pathways depending on the activation signals they receive and up-regulate glycolysis irrespective of oxygen levels in a phenomenon known as aerobic glycolysis $(37,38)$. Therefore, we examined whether S. aureus stimulation induced glycolysis in BMDMs. After stimulation with fixed S. aureus, M-CSF-derived BMDMs increased the expression of genes involved in glycolysis, including the transcription factor Hif1a and its target genes Slc2a1, encoding the glucose transporter GLUT1, and Slc16a3, encoding the lactate transporter MCT4 (Fig. 4A and fig. S4A). GLUT1 was previously reported to be the primary glucose transporter in classically activated Macs $(39,40)$, whereas MCT4 is required for optimal inflammatory response in lipopolysaccharide (LPS)-activated Macs (41). Metabolic assays revealed increased glycolysis coupled with decreased mitochondrial function (Fig. 4, B and C, fig. S4B). In addition, HIF-1a localized to the nucleus in BMDMs stimulated with S. aureus, similarly indicating a switch to aerobic glycolysis (fig. S4C). The switch was dependent on TLR signaling, as indicated by a reduced glycolytic capacity in Myd88 ${ }^{-/-}$BMDMs (fig. S4, D and E).

Next, we examined whether dermal Macs alter their metabolism in a similar manner during S. aureus skin infection. To confirm that the site of infection was hypoxic, only one ear of each wt mouse was infected, whereas the other ear remained uninfected. At 1 dpi , the mice were administered the hypoxia marker pimonidazole (Hypoxyprobe) 1.5 hours before euthanasia. Confocal microscopy revealed increased regions positive for pimonidazole in the infected ear. Furthermore, pimonidazole staining was increased primarily at the site of infection ("S" in Fig. 4D), whereas sites located at the periphery of infection ("P" in Fig. 4D) had reduced signal intensity (Fig. 4, D and E). In addition, mitochondrial dyes revealed alterations in both mitochondria mass and membrane potential in Macs at 1 dpi (Fig. 4F). In line with a hypoxic microenvironment, dermal Macs sorted from infected mice showed increased expression of Hif1a, Slc2a1, and Slc16a3 (Fig. 4G and fig. S4F) and nuclear HIF-1a protein (Fig. 4, H and I). In Cxcr4 ${ }^{\text {CreERT2/+ }}$ :R26 ${ }^{-/ / / / / / / / /}$ mice, we found that both prior resident and newly infiltrating Macs up-regulated Slc2a1 and Slc16a3 expression at 1 dpi (fig. S4G).

Collectively, these results indicated that dermal Macs increase glycolysis in response to both local hypoxia and S. aureus skin infection. To ascertain the importance of these metabolic changes, we analyzed conditional HIF-1a ko mice (Hif1a ${ }^{\text {fl/fl }}$ crossed to either LysM ${ }^{\text {Cre/+ }}$ or $\mathrm{Cx} 3 \mathrm{Cr} 1^{\text {Cre/+ }}$ mice, referred to as $\mathrm{Hif1a}^{-/-}$). ## GM-CSF-derived Macs do not require HIF-1 $\alpha$ for full activation

The reduced expression of Slc2a1 and Slc16a3 in Csf2rb ${ }^{-/-}$dermal Macs prompted us to examine the metabolic differences between BMDMs generated in vitro using either M-CSF or GM-CSF. The use of BMDMs was required here because the number of dermal macs that can be sorted from the skin is insufficient for many metabolic assays. Whereas both growth factors are commonly used to generate Macs in vitro $(44,45)$, the populations differed markedly in terms of their transcriptome (fig. S5A). Several inflammatory genes were increased in control GM-CSF BMDMs (fig. S5B), whereas no difference in the expression of Ifnb was observed, and GM-CSF Macs did not express Il10 in steady state (fig. S5B). We have previously found that GM-CSF-derived Macs are a potent source of IFN$\beta$ in response to group B Streptococcus (44). This was also true in regards to S. aureus, because only GM-CSF BMDMs secreted detectable amounts of IFN- $\beta$ (fig. S5D). However, M-CSF Macs could be induced to produce IFN- $\beta$, if M-CSF was replaced by GM-CSF before stimulation (referred to as GM-CSF-pulsed; fig. S5C). In this case, both Ifnb gene expression and secreted IFN- $\beta$ protein were boosted to levels comparable to BMDMs that had been cultured with GM-CSF for 6 days (fig. S5D). In addition, steady-state GM-CSF BMDMs displayed increased baseline glycolysis (Fig. 5, A to C) as well as higher expression of glutamine transporters and glutamine uptake (fig. S5E), indicative of increased metabolic activity in these cells.

To examine whether these groups also differed in terms of their HIF-1 $\alpha$ requirement to induce glycolysis, we generated BMDMs from wt and Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$mice using either M-CSF or GM-CSF. MCSF Hif1 $\alpha^{-/-}$BMDMs had reduced expression of Slc2a1 compared with wt cells, used less glucose, and secreted less lactate after both stimulation with hf or infection with live S. aureus (Fig. 5D).

Moreover, Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$BMDMs were impaired in mounting an effective inflammatory response as determined by nitric oxide (NO) and IL-1 $\beta$ production (Fig. 5E). In contrast, BMDMs generated in GMCSF displayed a HIF-1 $\alpha$-independent phenotype. No difference in Slc2a1 expression was observed, whereas glucose uptake and lactate secretion were comparable to wt cells (Fig. 5F), as were NO and IL$1 \beta$ production (Fig. 5G). In addition, extracellular flux analysis revealed that M-CSF Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$BMDMs had reduced glycolysis, whereas HIF-1 $\alpha$ deficiency did not affect glycolysis in GM-CSF BMDMs (fig. S5F). Last, wt M-CSF Macs treated with the HIF-1 $\alpha$ inhibitor CAY10585 produced less NO in response to live S. aureus, whereas GM-CSF Macs were unaffected (fig. S5G). Moreover, pulsing of Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$M-CSF-derived BMDMs with GM-CSF for 24 hours (setup depicted in fig. S5C) was sufficient to overrule HIF-1 $\alpha$ deficiency and resulted in comparable levels of lactate secretion upon S. aureus stimulation in wt and HIF1a ${ }^{-/-}$BMDMs (fig. S5H). To complement the above data, we incubated human induced pluripotent stem cells (IPSC)-derived Macs in either human (hu) M-CSF or huGM-CSF. After an incubation period of 3 days, the cells were harvested and stimulated with live S. aureus in the presence or absence of a HIF-1 $\alpha$ inhibitor, and their glycolytic capacity was measured. Infected IPSC-derived Macs up-regulated glycolysis as compared with controls regardless of whether they were cultured in huM-CSF or huGM-CSF. However, only the cells cultured in huM-CSF were susceptible to HIF-1 $\alpha$ inhibition, as indicated by reduced extracellular acidification rate. In contrast, and in agreement with our BMDM data, IPSC-derived Macs cultured in huGM-CSF did not require HIF-1 $\alpha$ for optimal glycolysis (fig. S5I).

Because HIF-1 $\alpha$ is a key player in the cellular response to hypoxia $(46,47)$, we wondered whether GM-CSF-derived Macs would maintain their HIF-1 $\alpha$-independent phenotype even under lowoxygen conditions. We analyzed wt BMDMs on an extracellular flux analyzer placed inside a hypoxia chamber ( $1 \%$ oxygen). GMCSF BMDMs incubated for 2 hours at $1 \%$ oxygen had increased glycolysis compared with M-CSF-derived Macs, suggesting an enhanced ability to respond to hypoxic conditions (fig. S5J). Moreover, whereas $1 \%$ oxygen up-regulated the expression of Slc2a1 and Slc16a3 irrespective of whether wt BMDMs were cultured in M-CSF or GM-CSF, only GM-CSF Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$, but not MCSF Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$, BMDMs up-regulated both genes in low oxygen (Fig. 5, H to J). Lastly, M-CSF Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$BMDMs incubated in low oxygen took up less glucose and secreted less lactate than wt cells upon stimulation (Fig. 5K and fig. S5K), whereas Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$and wt BMDMs cultured in GM-CSF did not differ (Fig. 5L and fig. S5K). As was the case in normoxic conditions, short-term pulsing of MCSF Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$BMDMs with GM-CSF rescued the impairment of glycolysis in these cells (Fig. 5M). Thus, GM-CSF Macs did not require HIF-1 $\alpha$ for glycolysis up-regulation even under lowoxygen conditions. However, it remained puzzling that GM-CSF Macs did not require HIF-1 $\alpha$ and that dermal Macs were reprogrammed by GM-CSF during infection, yet bacterial clearance was impaired in Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$mice at 5 dpi . We hypothesized that HIF-1 $\alpha$ was required at the very early stages of infection before GM-CSF imprinted dermal Macs. Accordingly, we infected mice for 6 hours with S. aureus. Whereas Mac numbers did not differ in wt and Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$mice in steady state (fig. S5L), Mac numbers were reduced in Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$mice 6 hours after infection (fig. S5M). ## S. aureus-induced itaconate mediates infection resolution

Besides increased glycolysis, a metabolic hallmark of inflammatory Macs is the modulation of the tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle and the accumulation of metabolites that exhibit cytokine-like roles in immunity (48). Therefore, we analyzed the kinetics of TCA cycle metabolites of S. aureus-stimulated BMDMs. We found lactate, pyruvate, succinate, fumarate, itaconate, and malate all to be increased by 24 hours when the Mac autonomous response was at its peak (fig. S6, A and D). In the case of itaconate, this seemed intriguing because it has previously been suggested as a negative regulator of TLR-mediated cytokine activation (49). Accordingly, we further explored itaconate and its regulation and found that $S$. aureus induced the expression of Irg1 and the synthesis of itaconate in both M-CSF and GM-CSF BMDMs in a HIF-1 $\alpha$-independent manner (Fig. 6, A and B and fig. S6, B and C). Irg1 expression and itaconate synthesis were induced by GM-CSF BMDMs already at baseline (Fig. 6, A and B, and fig. S6E). Because itaconate is derived from cis-aconitate in the TCA cycle, its baseline production likely explains the increased glutamine uptake in GM-CSF BMDMs (fig. S5E). Stable isotope labeling using ${ }^{13} \mathrm{C}$ glucose revealed that itaconate was, in part, derived from glucose in steady state as well as in stimulated GM-CSF and M-CSF BMDMs (fig. S6F). We next wondered what was driving Irg1 expression in steady-state GM-CSF BMDMs. IFN- $\beta$ has previously been reported to induce itaconate in LPS-activated BMDMs (50).

However, the steady-state expression of Ifnb was similar in MCSF and GM-CSF BMDMs (fig. S6G). Also, no difference in steady-state Irg1 expression was observed between wt and Ifnar ${ }^{-/-}$GM-CSF BMDMs (fig. S6H), indicating mediators beyond IFN- $\beta$.

Instead, GM-CSF BMDMs had increased expression of Tnf (fig. S6I) and secreted TNF protein in steady state (fig. S6J). GM-CSF BMDMs from Tnfr1 ${ }^{-/-}$mice had impaired expression of Irg1 compared with wt cells (fig. S6K), suggesting that autocrine TNF signaling may drive itaconate synthesis.

To assess the function of itaconate in regulating Mac activity during infection, we analyzed the cytokine response to S. aureus (and LPS) in $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$M-CSF or GM-CSF BMDMs. IRG1 deficiency in M-CSF BMDMs resulted in similar IL-6 but increased TNF and IL-1 $\beta$ levels (fig. S6L). However, both TNF and IL-1 $\beta$ were reduced in $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$GM-CSF BMDMs in response to S. aureus as compared with wt BMDM (fig. S6M). Accordingly, in contrast to the situation in M-CSF Macs, itaconate is required for an optimal response to $S$. aureus by GM-CSF Macs.

Next, we explored whether itaconate was relevant during $S$. aureus skin infection. Dermal Macs up-regulated the expression of Irg1 after infection (Fig. 6C), whereas itaconate was detected in Macs sorted from wt mice at 7 dpi (Fig. 6D). To determine the relevance of this, we infected wt and $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice with S. aureus. In
contrast to Hif1a ${ }^{-/-}$mice, $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice did not have reduced CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs at 6 hours post-infection (Fig. 6E). Similarly, at 1 dpi, both groups had comparable bacterial loads (Fig. 6F) and Mac frequencies (fig. S6N). However, at 5 dpi, PML and bacteria were increased in $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$as compared with wt mice (Fig. 6, H and I) without differences in Mac frequencies (Fig. 6G and fig. S6O). To investigate preferential itaconate utilization by Mac subsets, we analyzed BM chimeras with wt or $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$BM transferred to $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$or wt mice. S. aureus infection 8 weeks after transfer revealed, at 5 dpi , an increased bacterial burden in the wt-to$\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$group as compared with $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$-to-wt and wt-to-wt conditions (Fig. 6J). To exclude that direct antimicrobial properties of itaconate affected in vivo infection (51), the growth of S. aureus cultured in LB media with or without itaconic acid was analyzed and found not to differ (Fig. 6K).

Next, we applied 4-octyl itaconate (4-OI) directly to the ears of $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice before infection (Fig. 6L). Early after infection (1 dpi), bacterial numbers were similar in wt and $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice that had received either 4-OI or dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) (Fig. 6M). At 5 dpi, 4-OI-treated $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice had reduced Macs (Fig. 6N) and bacterial burden as compared with infected $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice without 4-OI, whereas skin PML were almost completely absent (Fig. 6O). In addition, the anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 was increased in tissue lysates from these mice (Fig. 6P). To establish the source of IL-10, we treated mice as depicted in Fig. 6L, generated single-cell suspensions, and performed intracellular IL-10 staining. We identified CD4 and regulatory T cells, as well as DCs and CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs, as sources of IL-10 (Fig. 6, Q and R) (markers used to identify the analyzed populations are depicted in fig. S6, P and Q).

Last, to gain insight into the profound effect of 4-OI on infection resolution, dermal Macs were sorted at 1 dpi. Macs from DMSOtreated $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice had increased expression of several inflammatory genes as compared with Macs sorted from infected wt mice. The expression levels of these genes were restored to wt levels in dermal Macs sorted from 4-OI-treated $\operatorname{Irg1}^{-/-}$mice, whereas 4 OI also blunted the expression of Il1b (Fig. 6S). Collectively, these results indicate that itaconate is broadly required to modulate inflammatory gene expression by Macs during infection.

## DISCUSSION

In mucocutaneous barriers, direct contact between dermal Macs and S. aureus occurs on a daily basis. Tightly regulated response patterns in Macs are required to encounter these frequent challenges while preserving long-term tissue homeostasis.

In the steady state, dermal Macs require M-CSF for at-site differentiation and maintenance (9). However, in bacterial infection, GM-CSF, which is initially derived from skin resident $\gamma \delta \mathrm{T}$ cells, diverts dermal Macs away from a homeostatic M-CSF program for the acquisition of distinct immunometabolic properties. With the help of fate mapping, we found that, although GM-CSF acted on both resident and newly recruited Macs, the latter responded much more robustly. Our BM transfer experiments supported this because the transfer of Csf2rb-deficient BM decreased bacterial resistance in wt mice. This association of recent history of provenance and initial conditioning in a homeostatic versus an inflammatory environment correlated with differences in localization. GM-CSF was nonredundant in this context because dermal Macs sorted from GM-CSF receptor-deficient mice failed to mount a sufficient inflammatory response, because they were incapable of clearing the infection in a timely fashion. Furthermore, we identify GM-CSF as a key regulator of innate immune memory in the skin, although other factors are likely to essentially contribute.

Whereas a central role of intrinsic metabolic changes in immune cell activation has been firmly established in recent years, the bulk of the data have been generated in vitro (38). Whereas these approaches provide valuable molecular information (particularly because, due to low cell numbers, dermal Macs cannot be used in many metabolic assays, e.g., Seahorse analysis), they fall short of depicting the complexity of intercellular networks, especially during infection where the immune cell composition and single-cell states alter dynamically. As a notable example, the transcription factor HIF-1 $\alpha$ has a central role in homeostatic dermal Macs for initiating the immune response in staphylococcal infection, in line with its established central position in allowing for the glycolytic switch in inflammatory Macs in vitro. Yet, by 1 dpi , GM-CSF signaling allowed Macs to bypass the requirement for HIF-1 $\alpha$, even under hypoxic conditions. The central role of GM-CSF in fine-tuning Mac metabolism is underlined by our unexpected observation, that it alone induces Irg1 expression and itaconate generation in vitro, in a partly TNF-dependent fashion. It seems an intriguing hypothesis that GM-CSF discretely conditions subsets of Macs in one tissue for optimal adaptation to metabolic restrictions and to the chance of infectious encounters. Our data on GM-CSF-producing T cells approaching Macs in staphylococcal skin infection make this a highly plausible scenario. Whether and how the outlined axis of GM-CSF and itaconate affects resident Macs which require GMCSF in vivo, in particular alveolar Macs, remains to be explored.

Next to GM-CSF, we identified the formation of itaconate to be a potent tuning mechanism in staphylococcal infection. The immunomodulatory properties of itaconate have been previously appreciated, mostly using in vitro models $(49,50,52)$, with increasing evidence in vivo (53-56). BM chimeras revealed increased bacterial loads in the wt-to-Irg1 $1^{-/-}$group as compared with controls, whereas no significant difference was observed when $\operatorname{Irg} 1^{-/-}$BM was transferred to wt animals. The combination of approaches, including bone marrow chimeras, applied in this study suggests that itaconate synthesis by prior resident Macs as compared with incoming Macs may be particularly relevant during infection resolution. The mechanistic reason for a differential requirement of GM-CSF signaling and itaconate synthesis by prior resident and newly recruited Macs is currently unclear and will require further study.

The fact that a slight delay in Macs activation results in substantially increased inflammation 5 days later strongly supports a model where IRG1 is critical for an early robust cytokine response by GMCSF-primed Macs involving itaconate as an activity-promoting metabolite. In line with this, impairment of staphylococcal infection resolution in $\operatorname{Irg} 1^{-/-}$mice could be compensated for by the local application of 4-OI directly to the skin before infection with complex Mac reprogramming. The potential of 4-OI in directing inflammation at the site of infection offers translational perspectives for exploiting immunometabolic Macs properties in tissue infection, thus expanding the therapeutic potential of metabolites $(57,58)$.

We show that Macs adopt a GM-CSF-dependent transcriptional program during infection. However, it should be noted that we cannot conclude that M-CSF receptor down-regulation equates to nonfunctionality in dermal macs. In addition, we show that GM-CSF-conditioned Macs exhibit a HIF-1 $\alpha$-independent phenotype, yet the precise mechanism for this is currently unknown and requires further investigation. We observe that local 4-OI treatment modulates gene expression in Macs. However, it is likely to also affect other immune as well as stromal cells in the skin. Further investigation is needed to understand the impact of 4-OI on cells beyond dermal Macs. This is especially vital if exploring the therapeutic potential of 4-OI in skin diseases. Last, our work indicates a potentially essential role for type 1 IFNs which peak late in infection, when bacteria have already largely been cleared from the tissue. Currently, underlying regulatory mechanisms and the functional consequences of the late type 1 IFNs are unclear and should be the subject of future studies.

In summary, we demonstrate that, during staphylococcal skin infection, dermal Macs are subjected to local hypoxia and TLR activation. This engages several sequential and interwoven signaling pathways, both in resident and in newly recruited Macs, all of which are critical for infection resolution. This Mac program represents a bottleneck in antimicrobial resistance, inflammation control, and temporal tissue immune memory and may contain molecular targets for therapeutic interventions.

## MATERIALS AND METHODS

## Study design

This study investigated dermal Mac intrinsic signaling pathways and microenvironmental changes in S. aureus skin infection. High-dimensional flow cytometry and whole-mount microscopy were used to elucidate alterations in the skin immune cell composition during infection. Transcriptome analysis of sorted CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{HL}}$ dermal Macs revealed the up-regulation of Csf2rb and alterations in core metabolic machinery during infection. The importance of GM-CSF signaling for Mac activation was interrogated via qPCR analysis of fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS)-sorted Macs from wt and $\operatorname{Csf} 2 r b^{-/-}$mice. Cre-loxP-mediated ablation of Csf2rb and Hif1a as well as full ko models ( $\operatorname{Csf} 2 r b^{-/-}$and $\operatorname{Irg} 1^{-/-}$) were used to determine the importance of these factors on infection dynamics. Group sizes and number of repetitions are indicated in the figure legends.

## Bacterial strains

## Mouse model of intradermal skin infection

All procedures were approved by the Regional Commission of Freiburg, Baden - Württemberg. About $10^{7}$ colony-forming units (CFUs) of S. aureus in $10 \mu \mathrm{l}$ of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) were intradermally injected [30-gauge needle, U-100 insulin syringe (BD Biosciences)] into the ear pinnae of anesthetized mice. For reinfection experiments, $10^{7}$ CFUs of S. aureus in $10 \mu \mathrm{l}$ of PBS were intradermally injected into the same ear pinnae 3 weeks after the first infection. For experiments involving Hypoxyprobe, 100 mg of pimonidazole was dissolved in 1 ml of sterile saline solution and stored at $4^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$. wt mice infected for 1 day were injected with Hypoxyprobe intraperitoneally (ip) with a dose of $100 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{kg}$ body weight (bw) 1.5 hours before euthanasia. For local on-ear 4-OI application, a working solution of $500 \mu \mathrm{M}$ in DMSO was prepared. Using a pipette, $20 \mu \mathrm{l}$ of the working solution was added to each ear of anesthetized mice 1 day prior and again 1 hour before intradermal infection. DMSO alone served as a control. On the day of analysis, mice were euthanized, and the ears were removed at the hairline. One ear was used for immune cell phenotyping of skin tissue as described below. The other ear was used for the quantification of CFU counts. The ear was mechanically homogenized in 1 ml of PBS containing one tungsten bead using a TissueLyser (Qiagen) before being filtered through a $70-\mu \mathrm{m}$ cell strainer. CFUs in the homogenized skin tissue were determined by serial dilutions on blood agar plates.

## Bone marrow transplantation

Recipient mice were anesthetized using ketamine ( $100 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{kg}$ bw) and xylazine ( $10 \mathrm{mg} / \mathrm{kg}$ bw) by intraperitoneal injection. Bepanthen eye cream was applied to the eyes to protect them from dehydration. The animals were then placed in a "shielding device" ( $6-\mathrm{mm}$ steel plate) that protected the left ear from irradiation. The mice received one dose of irradiation at 9 grays (Gy) and then were injected with $10 \times 10^{6} \mathrm{BM}$ cells (in $100 \mu \mathrm{l}$ of PBS) via the tail vein. Eight weeks after transplantation, the mice were infected with S. aureus only in the left ear.

## Immune cell phenotyping of skin tissue

Whole mouse ears were subjected to enzymatic digestion as previously described (7). After digestion, samples were filtered with a $70-$ $\mu \mathrm{m}$ cell strainer (BD Biosciences), washed with FACS buffer (PBS +

2\% fetal calf serum +2 mM EDTA), and incubated with Fc block (anti-mouse CD16/32, Clone 93, BioLegend) for 5 min on ice. To quantify the $\%$ of skin neutrophils and CD64 ${ }^{\mathrm{Hi}}$ Macs in the tissue, the following antibodies were used: anti-mouse CD45 eFluor450 Clone 30-F11 (eBioscience), anti-mouse CD11b PE-Cy7 Clone M1/70(eBioscience), anti-mouse Ly6G FITC Clone 1A8 (BD Pharmingen), anti-mouse Ly6C PE Clone HK1.4 (Invitrogen), antimouse CD64 CloneX54-5/7.1 PerCP/Cy5.5 (BioLegend), antimouse CD11c APC Clone HL3 (BD Biosciences), and anti-mouse CD3e APC Clone 145-2C11 (BioLegend). Cell samples were analyzed with a 10-color flow cytometer (Gallios, Beckman Coulter) and the Kaluza software (version 1.5a, Beckman Coulter). For sorting of dermal Macs for RNA-seq analysis, the mouse ears were treated as above and stained with the following antibodies: anti-mouse CD11b PE-Cy7, anti-mouse CD45 eFluor450, antimouse CD11c APC, and anti-mouse CD64 PerCP/Cy5.5 (BioLegend), as well as anti-mouse CD3e PE, anti-mouse Ly6G FITC Clone 1A8 (BD Pharmingen), anti-mouse NK1.1 Biotin Clone PK136, and anti-mouse Siglec F Biotin Clone ES22-10D8 (Miltenyi Biotec). After a washing step, the cells were incubated for 20 mins on ice with Streptavidin PE (eBioscience).

For experiments using tetramethylrhodamine (TMRM) and MitoTracker Green (both Thermo Fisher Scientific), the cell suspension was stained as described above. Next, the cells were incubated with 20 nM MitoTracker Green or 50 nM TMRM at $37^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for 20 min in the dark. After incubation, the cells were washed and analyzed.

For intracellular cytokine staining [GM-CSF, IL-10, IL-17(A), IL-2, IL-22, IFN- $\gamma$, TNF, and IL-4], cells were restimulated in ionomycin ( $500 \mathrm{ng} / \mathrm{ml}$, Invitrogen) and phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate- containing ( $50 \mathrm{ng} / \mathrm{ml}$, Applichem) medium (RPMI 1640 complete medium) supplemented with Golgi-Plug and GolgiStop (both 1:1000; BD Biosciences) at $37^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for 4 hours. After restimulation, surface labeling was performed by incubating the cells with antibodies in PBS at $4^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for 20 min . Next, cells were fixed and permeabilized with $100 \mu \mathrm{l}$ of BD Cytofix/Cytoperm solution at $4^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ for 20 min . Afterward, cells were washed and labeled intracellularly at $4^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in a permeabilization buffer overnight. Data acquisition was performed on a 5L Cytek Aurora (Cytek), and Fcs files were analyzed using FlowJo software (Tree Star). Antibodies are listed in Supplementary Materials and Methods.

## High-dimensional analysis

High-dimensional analysis was performed as described previously (60). In short, raw files were preprocessed using FlowJo. Compensated CD45 ${ }^{+}$, live single cells were exported from FlowJo and imported into R studio. Transformation and percentile normalization were performed to obtain values between 0 and 1. This was followed by dimensionality reduction and visualization by UMAP. A FlowSOM algorithm was used for automated clustering and meta-clustering, followed by expert-guided merging of some clusters using the information from the heatmap with median expression values and the UMAP with overlayed expression values.

## Immunofluorescence analysis of whole-tissue mounts

For immunofluorescence analysis of whole ear mounts, mice were euthanized, and ears were cut at the hairline. The ears were split into dorsal and ventral parts and incubated in 24 -well plates overnight in1 ml of $1 \%$ paraformaldehyde at $4^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ in the dark, then incubated overnight in wash buffer ( $1 \%$ bovine serum albumin, $0.1 \%$ Triton $\mathrm{X}-100,4^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$ ). After mechanical removal of cartilage, the ears were stained in $200 \mu \mathrm{l}$ of wash buffer overnight at $4^{\circ} \mathrm{C}$, then washed three times ( 5 min on a rocking table). The ears were then mounted on microscopy slides in ProLong Diamond, allowed to dry overnight, and analyzed on a Zeiss LSM 880 AiryScan confocal microscope using a $20 \times, 40 \times$, or $63 \times$ objective. ImageJ was used to quantify the signal intensity of whole mounts stained with anti-pimonidazole antibodies. For 3D reconstruction, Imaris cell imaging software was used.

## RNA-seq

BMDMs in M-CSF cultures were defined as being for CD11b and F4/80. In GM-CSF cultures, the BMDMs were defined as CD115 ${ }^{\text {Hi }}$ and MHC-II ${ }^{\mathrm{Lo}}$. After sorting, BMDMs were seeded at $1 \times 10^{6}$ cells per well into six-well plates in medium with M-CSF or GM-CSF (20 $\mathrm{ng} / \mathrm{ml}$ ). The next day, the BMDMs were stimulated with $1 \times 10^{7} \mathrm{ml}$ of hf $S$. aureus for 4 hours. The cells were washed, harvested with gentle scraping, pelleted, and lysed in $350 \mu \mathrm{l}$ of RLT buffer (Qiagen) with $1 \% \beta$-mercaptoethanol. RNA was extracted with RNeasy MicroKit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Qiagen). Sample processing was performed by CeGaT (Center for Genomics and Transcriptomics, Tübingen, Germany). Library preparation was performed with 100 ng of quality-controlled RNA using the TruSeq Stranded mRNA kit (Illumina). A Q30 value of $94.4 \%$ was reached. Demultiplexing of the sequencing reads was performed with Illumina bcl2fastq (2.19). Adapters were trimmed with Skewer (version 0.2.2) (61). Trimmed raw reads were aligned to reference genome mm10 using STAR (version 2.5.2b) (62). Differential expression analysis between groups was performed with DESeq2 (63) in R (R Core Team 2015).

For dermal Macs, wt mice were infected for 1, 5, and 7 days or left uninfected. Dermal Macs were sorted from the ear and lysed in 350 $\mu \mathrm{l}$ of RLT buffer supplemented with $1 \% \beta$-mercaptoethanol. The RNA was extracted using the RNeasy MicroKit according to the manufacturer's instructions (Qiagen). Paired-end sequencing of the quality-controlled library was carried out with NovaSeq 6000. Demultiplexing of sequenced reads was performed with Illumina bcl2fastq (version 2.19) and adapter trimming with Cutadapt (64). The raw reads were subsequently mapped to the mm10 murine reference genome with STAR (Version 2.5.2b) (62) and counted with feature counts (65). The obtained counted reads were processed using the DESeq2 package (63).

Heatmaps were generated with Morpheus (https://software. broadinstitute.org/morpheus/). GO enrichment analysis was performed using the PANTHER classification database (66) on regulated ( $P_{\text {adj }}<0.05$ and $>2 \log _{2}$ fold change expression) genes. Volcano plots were generated in R on genes with a $P<0.05$ and $>2 \log _{2}$ fold change expression. Datasets have been uploaded to the GEO platform (accession number: GSE174676).

## Quantification and statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed with GraphPad Prism (GraphPad software, Version 8 or 9). For the comparison of the two groups, an unpaired two-tailed Student's $t$ test or Mann-Whitney test was applied. For multiple comparisons, one- or two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, followed by Tukey's multiple comparison or Dunnett's multiple comparison tests. Differences were
considered statistically significant when ${ }^{*} P \leq 0.05,{ }^{* *} P \leq 0.01$, ${ }^{* * *} P \leq 0.001$, and ${ }^{* * * *} P \leq 0.0001$. Data are presented as means $\pm$ SEM if not stated otherwise.
